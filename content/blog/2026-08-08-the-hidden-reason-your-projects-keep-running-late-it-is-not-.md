You have missed the deadline again. The client is unhappy, the team is frustrated, and when you dig into what went wrong, everyone points at everyone else. The developer was waiting on the designer. The designer was waiting on approval. The account manager forgot to chase the approval because they were buried in emails. Sound familiar? Most project delays get blamed on scope creep, under-resourcing, or poor planning. Those things matter, but there is a quieter culprit that almost nobody talks about: the dead time between tasks, when work sits in someone's inbox or Slack thread waiting for a human to manually pass it along to the next person. This is the glue work — and it is silently killing your delivery timelines.

## The Real Bottleneck Is the Hand-Off, Not the Work Itself

Think about a typical project at your firm. A brief gets approved. Someone needs to create a task in your project management tool. Someone else needs to be notified in Slack. A folder needs to be created in Google Drive. A kickoff meeting needs to be scheduled. None of these steps are difficult, but each one requires a human to remember to do them, find the time to do them, and do them correctly.

Research from McKinsey suggests that knowledge workers spend roughly 20% of their working week — a full day every week — on activities like searching for information, chasing updates, and coordinating hand-offs. In a ten-person team, that is two full-time salaries worth of effort going into the gaps between the actual work. At an average UK knowledge worker salary of around £40,000, that is £80,000 a year in lost productive time for a team of ten — before you factor in a single delayed project or a single unhappy client.

The hand-off problem is especially brutal for office and professional services teams — law firms, consultancies, agencies, growing SMEs — because your projects are complex, involve multiple tools, and depend on precise sequencing. When a contract is signed, twelve things need to happen in the right order. When they do not, the delay is not one day. It compounds.

## What "Glue Work" Actually Looks Like in Practice

Let us make this concrete. Imagine a small marketing consultancy with fifteen staff. When a new client project is approved, a project manager needs to:

- Create a project in Asana with the right template
- Add the client to the CRM with relevant tags
- Set up a shared Slack channel and invite the right people
- Send a welcome email to the client
- Create a Google Drive folder with the standard structure
- Schedule a kickoff call and send a calendar invite

On a good day, a capable project manager can do all of this in about forty-five minutes. But it rarely happens on a good day. It happens on a Tuesday afternoon when that same project manager is on two calls, handling a revision request from another client, and trying to eat lunch. The new client setup gets done in fragments over two days. The developer starts without the brief because the Drive folder was not shared. The welcome email goes out late. The client notices.

Now multiply this by every new project — perhaps thirty or forty a year — and you start to see how the aggregate delay adds up to missed revenue targets, strained client relationships, and a team that perpetually feels behind.

## How AI Agents Fix the Glue Without Replacing Your Team

An AI agent in this context is not a chatbot or a futuristic robot. It is a piece of software that watches for a trigger — say, a deal moving to "Won" in your CRM — and then automatically carries out a sequence of actions across your other tools. Think of it as a very reliable, very fast team member whose only job is to do the hand-offs correctly, every single time, in under sixty seconds.

Here is what that looks like for the marketing consultancy above. An AI automation workflow — built using tools like Make, Zapier, or a custom agent — detects when a deal is marked "Won" in HubSpot. Within moments, it creates a project in Asana from the correct template, generates a Google Drive folder with the right structure and shares it with the project team, opens a Slack channel and populates it with the client brief, sends a personalised welcome email to the client, and logs a follow-up task for the project manager to schedule the kickoff call. All of this happens before the project manager has finished reading the notification that the deal closed.

One real-world example: a London-based management consultancy with twenty-two staff implemented this kind of automation for their client onboarding workflow. Previously, onboarding a new engagement took between two and three days from contract signature to the team being fully set up and briefed. After automation, that figure dropped to under two hours — and crucially, it now happens consistently, without variation depending on who is in the office or how busy the operations lead is. They estimated the change saved their team roughly six hours of coordination work per new client, which at their billing rates translated to recovering over £400 per onboarding in productive time that could be redirected to billable work.

## The Compounding Effect on Deadlines and Client Trust

Here is the thing that most project post-mortems miss: the delay caused by a late hand-off does not just cost you the hours it took to do the hand-off. It costs you the hours the next person in the chain was not working, because they did not know they were supposed to start yet. If your developer sits idle for a day waiting for a brief that never landed in the right folder, you have not lost one day. You have lost a day of development time plus the ripple effect on everything downstream.

Automating the hand-offs eliminates this compounding effect. When every stage transition triggers the next stage automatically — with the right people notified, the right documents in place, and the right tasks created — your team spends their time doing the work rather than chasing the work. Projects that used to slip by three or four days because of accumulated micro-delays start landing on time, not because your team suddenly got better at their jobs, but because the invisible friction has been removed.

There is also a client trust dimension that is easy to underestimate. Clients do not see your internal chaos. What they see is whether they got their welcome email promptly, whether the kickoff happened on time, and whether deliverables arrive when promised. Automation makes you look like a firm that has its act together — because in the ways that matter to clients, you now do.

## Conclusion

The reason your projects keep running late is probably not your people, your planning, or your ambition. It is the invisible seconds, minutes, and hours lost every time a piece of work moves from one person or tool to another and has to wait for a human to notice, remember, and act. AI agents that automate these hand-offs are not about replacing your team — they are about removing the friction that currently stops your team from doing what they are actually good at. Start by mapping one workflow where work regularly stalls between steps. Chances are, that single hand-off is costing you more than you realise.
